# Metaheuristics: GA, PSO, Simulated Annealing, ACO

Code: [`code/genetic_algorithm.py`](code/genetic_algorithm.py), [`code/particle_swarm.py`](code/particle_swarm.py), [`code/tsp_solver.py`](code/tsp_solver.py) (simulated annealing + ant colony optimization live in `tsp_solver.py` alongside a nearest-neighbor baseline).

## What problem class these solve
Exact algorithms (DP, greedy with a proven exchange argument, branch-and-bound) guarantee optimal answers but don't scale when the search space is combinatorially huge and has no exploitable structure — the canonical example is the **Traveling Salesman Problem**: exact solutions are O(n^2 * 2^n) (Held-Karp DP) or worse, intractable much past a few dozen cities. More generally: **non-convex, high-dimensional, or combinatorial optimization** problems where the objective has many local optima, no useful gradient (or the function is a black box), and brute force is exponential.

Metaheuristics trade the optimality guarantee for the ability to find a *good* (not provably best) solution in reasonable time, by combining exploration (searching broadly, escaping local optima) and exploitation (refining around promising solutions found so far). All four below follow that same exploration/exploitation tension, just with different mechanisms for it.

## Genetic Algorithm (GA)
Models optimization as biological evolution over a **population** of candidate solutions ("individuals" — here, `[x1, x2]` float vectors minimizing a toy objective). Each generation:
1. **Selection** — pick parents biased toward fitter individuals. `run_genetic_algorithm` in [`genetic_algorithm.py`](code/genetic_algorithm.py) uses roulette-wheel selection: convert cost to fitness via `1/(1+cost)` (so lower cost -> higher weight) and sample proportionally with `random.choices`.
2. **Crossover** — combine two parents into offspring, hoping to combine their good traits. `arithmetic_crossover` blends genes with a random `alpha`: `child = alpha*parent1 + (1-alpha)*parent2`.
3. **Mutation** — randomly perturb offspring to maintain diversity and avoid premature convergence. `gaussian_mutate` adds gaussian noise to each gene independently with probability `rate`, clamped to bounds.
4. Repeat for `generations` rounds, tracking the best individual seen.

Exploration comes from mutation and the randomness in selection/crossover; exploitation comes from fitter individuals being more likely to reproduce. No elitism in this implementation (the whole population is replaced each generation), so the *best-ever* individual is tracked separately from the population itself — a real gotcha: without elitism, a good solution found in generation 10 can be lost by generation 11 if it isn't tracked.

## Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)
Models optimization as a **swarm** of particles moving through the search space, each pulled toward two attractors: its own best-ever position and the swarm's global best-ever position. In [`particle_swarm.py`](code/particle_swarm.py), each particle has a `position` and `velocity`; every iteration, `run_particle_swarm` updates velocity as a weighted sum of three terms:
- **Inertia** — keep moving in the current direction (`inertia * particle.velocity`).
- **Cognitive** pull toward the particle's own best-known position (`cognitive_coeff * r1 * (particle.best_position - particle.position)`).
- **Social** pull toward the swarm's global best (`social_coeff * r2 * (global_best_position - particle.position)`).

The random coefficients `r1, r2` inject stochastic exploration. This implementation also **linearly decays inertia** from `inertia_start=0.9` to `inertia_end=0.4` over the run (more exploratory early, more exploitative/convergent late) and **clamps velocity magnitude** (`velocity_limit`) so particles can't fly off to infinity — both are refinements on top of the textbook fixed-inertia PSO.

## Simulated Annealing (SA)
Models optimization as cooling metal: a single solution (not a population) takes random local steps, and a **temperature** parameter controls how willing the search is to accept a *worse* move. `solve_tsp_simulated_annealing` in [`tsp_solver.py`](code/tsp_solver.py) works over TSP tours: a neighbor is generated by swapping two cities in the current tour, then accepted via the **Metropolis criterion** — always accept an improving move, accept a worsening move with probability `exp(-delta / temp)`. High temperature early means the search wanders and can climb out of a local optimum; as `temp` cools, it converges into hill-climbing/greedy behavior. Two cooling schedules are implemented: exponential decay (`temp *= cooling_rate`, fast) and Cauchy/fast annealing (`temp = initial_temp / (1 + cooling_rate * t)`, a heavier tail that keeps accepting worse moves longer — useful for escaping optima that exponential cooling would already have frozen out of).

## Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)
Models optimization as **ants depositing pheromone** on good paths. `solve_tsp_ant_colony` in [`tsp_solver.py`](code/tsp_solver.py) maintains a pheromone level `tau[i][j]` per edge plus a static heuristic desirability `eta[i][j] = 1/distance(i,j)`. Each iteration, every ant builds a full tour by probabilistically choosing its next city, weighted by `tau[i][j]**alpha * eta[i][j]**beta` (pheromone influence vs. "closer is more attractive" influence) over unvisited candidates. After all ants finish: pheromone **evaporates** (`tau *= (1 - rho)`, preventing unbounded reinforcement and stale convergence) and each ant **deposits** pheromone proportional to `1/tour_length` on the edges it used — so shorter tours reinforce their edges more over successive iterations, and the colony's shared "memory" gradually converges on good edges. This is fundamentally population-based (many ants per iteration) and stigmergic (ants only communicate indirectly, through pheromone left in the environment).

## When an interviewer might ask about these
Rare compared to core DSA (arrays, trees, graphs, DP) — not a standard whiteboard-coding topic. Comes up in:
- **"Design an optimizer" / numerical optimization roles** — e.g. hyperparameter search, scheduling, routing systems, or anything framed as "we have a huge search space and no exact algorithm scales."
- **ML-adjacent system design** — evolutionary/PSO hyperparameter tuning is a real production technique (an alternative to grid/random search or Bayesian optimization).
- **Operations research / logistics-flavored interviews** — vehicle routing, job scheduling are TSP-adjacent and these heuristics are the standard practical toolkit there.
- More often tested at the *conceptual* level ("what's the difference between exploration and exploitation, name a technique that balances them") than "implement PSO from scratch on a whiteboard."

## Trade-offs vs. exact algorithms
- **No optimality guarantee** — metaheuristics can get stuck in, or converge near, a local optimum; there's no proof the returned solution is the global best (contrast with DP/exact branch-and-bound, which are provably optimal when they terminate).
- **Hyperparameter-sensitive** — population size, mutation rate, cooling schedule, pheromone evaporation rate, etc. all need tuning per problem; a poor choice can mean slow convergence or premature convergence to a bad local optimum. Exact algorithms have no such tuning surface.
- **No tight runtime bound** — "run for N iterations/generations" is a practical stopping rule, not a proof of convergence within that budget; exact algorithms have known worst-case complexity.
- **Scale to huge/ill-structured search spaces** — this is the entire point: they remain usable (though not guaranteed-optimal) on problems where exact methods are outright infeasible (TSP past ~20-40 cities for exact DP, continuous non-convex objectives with no closed form). They also don't require the objective to be differentiable or even analytically known (black-box/simulation-based objectives are fine).
- **Anytime behavior** — can be stopped early and still return the best solution found so far, which exact branch-and-bound-style algorithms generally can't offer mid-run (except specifically anytime variants).
